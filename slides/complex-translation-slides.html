complex translation

We now put translation to work on real, messy, natural-language arguments.

One minute. This deck assumes the translation skills from last time and puts them to the test on arguments nobody wrote with a truth table in mind.

the plan

Three steps, applied to each argument in turn:

  1. write the argument in premise-conclusion form
  2. translate the argument into the language of propositional logic
  3. use a truth table to determine whether the argument is valid

Same three steps as always — the only thing that changes is that the English is now dense enough that step 1 takes real work.

worked example: the burglar

A closing-argument style example — good for showing that ‘if…then…if…then…’ chains are just nested conditionals.

the argument

My client is not the burglar. For if there are no signs of forced entry, then, if my client had been the burglar, she would have had an accomplice inside the building. But we can discard that option, as no one in the building could have known my client before today. Nor are there any signs of forced entry.

Read it aloud once. The conclusion is announced first (‘my client is not the burglar’) — worth flagging, since English often states the conclusion before the premises.

in premise-conclusion form

  1. If there are no signs of forced entry, then if my client had been the burglar, then she would have had an accomplice inside the building.
  2. She did not have an accomplice inside the building.
  3. There are no signs of forced entry.
  1. My client is not the burglar.

Premise 1 is a conditional whose consequent is itself a conditional — that nesting is the whole challenge of this example.

translation key

\(p\): There are signs of forced entry

\(q\): My client is the burglar

\(r\): My client had an accomplice inside the building

Now translate each line of the argument yourself:

Double-check the key before typing — with three variables sharing a slide, it is easy to swap \(p\) and \(r\).

translate it: premise 1

key: \(p\): signs of forced entry    \(q\): client is the burglar    \(r\): client had an accomplice

The nested conditional from the argument slide, unpacked: negate p for ‘no signs’, then the whole ‘if q then r’ becomes the consequent.

translate it: premise 2

key: \(p\): signs of forced entry    \(q\): client is the burglar    \(r\): client had an accomplice

translate it: premise 3

key: \(p\): signs of forced entry    \(q\): client is the burglar    \(r\): client had an accomplice

translate it: the conclusion

key: \(p\): signs of forced entry    \(q\): client is the burglar    \(r\): client had an accomplice

the argument, fully translated

  1. \(\neg p \to (q \to r)\)
  2. \(\neg r\)
  3. \(\neg p\)
  1. \(\neg q\)

Recap slide — the four answers just checked live, gathered into the finished argument.

testing validity

No row makes all three premises true and the conclusion false — the argument is valid.

worked example: mind and brain

This one has a sting in its tail — unlike the burglar argument, it turns out to be invalid. Good for showing that the method finds invalidity just as readily as validity.

the argument

If the mind is the same thing as the brain, then the mind is made of matter only if the brain is made of matter. Unless the mind is mortal, it is not made of matter. The brain is made of matter. Therefore, the mind is not the same thing as the brain.

‘Only if’ and ‘unless’ both appear here — both were covered last time, so this is also a review of those two phrases.

in premise-conclusion form

  1. If the mind is the same thing as the brain, then the mind is made of matter only if the brain is made of matter.
  2. Unless the mind is mortal, it is not made of matter.
  3. The brain is made of matter.
  1. The mind is not the same thing as the brain.

Ask the room to paraphrase premises 1 and 2 out loud before moving to the key — ‘only if’ and ‘unless’ are exactly the phrases that trip people up under time pressure.

translation key

\(p\): the mind is the same thing as the brain

\(q\): the mind is made of matter

\(r\): the brain is made of matter

\(t\): the mind is mortal

Now translate each line of the argument yourself:

Four variables this time — worth pointing out that \(s\) is skipped in favor of \(t\) purely so it doesn’t get confused with ‘the same thing’.

translate it: premise 1

key: \(p\): mind=brain    \(q\): mind is matter    \(r\): brain is matter    \(t\): mind is mortal

‘Only if’ inside the consequent becomes its own conditional q → r, matching last session’s ‘only if’ slide exactly.

translate it: premise 2

key: \(p\): mind=brain    \(q\): mind is matter    \(r\): brain is matter    \(t\): mind is mortal

‘Unless’ becomes ‘if not’ — same paraphrase as last time’s unless slide.

translate it: premise 3

key: \(p\): mind=brain    \(q\): mind is matter    \(r\): brain is matter    \(t\): mind is mortal

translate it: the conclusion

key: \(p\): mind=brain    \(q\): mind is matter    \(r\): brain is matter    \(t\): mind is mortal

the argument, fully translated

  1. \(p \to (q \to r)\)
  2. \(\neg t \to \neg q\)
  3. \(r\)
  1. \(\neg p\)

Recap slide — the four answers just checked live, gathered into the finished argument.

testing validity

Let the room run the table before revealing anything — this is the case where it does not come out valid, and it lands better if they find that themselves.

finding a counterexample

  • to make the conclusion \(\neg p\) false, we need \(p\) true
  • premise 3 already forces \(r\) true
  • setting \(q\) and \(t\) both false keeps premises 1 and 2 true as well
  • assignment (\(p, q, r, t\)) = (T, F, T, F): every premise is true, the conclusion is false

The argument is invalid.

A nice moment to stress that ‘sounds compelling in English’ is not the same as ‘valid’ — this argument reads persuasively but the truth table finds the gap.

worked example: the Argus deal

Adapted from Quine. Five variables and 32 rows — too many to fill by hand, which is exactly the point: this is where the truth-table method still works even when brute-force table-filling doesn’t.

the argument

If Jones is ill or Smith is away, then unless Robinson comes to his senses, neither will the Argus deal be concluded nor will the directors declare a dividend. Consequently, if Smith is away and Robinson does not come to his senses, the Argus deal will not be concluded.

This argument has just two sentences: one premise, one conclusion (flagged by ‘consequently’). Everything else is packed inside those two sentences.

in premise-conclusion form

  1. If Jones is ill or Smith is away, then unless Robinson comes to his senses, neither will the Argus deal be concluded nor will the directors declare a dividend.
  1. If Smith is away and Robinson does not come to his senses, then the Argus deal will not be concluded.

Worth pausing here — students often expect an argument to have several premises. This one has exactly one, and it does all the work.

translation key

\(p\): Jones is ill

\(q\): Smith is away

\(r\): Robinson comes to his senses

\(s\): the Argus deal is completed

\(t\): the directors declare a dividend

Now translate the premise and the conclusion yourself:

‘Neither … nor …’ and ‘unless’ both reappear here, now nested three levels deep inside one sentence. Take it phrase by phrase.

translate it: the premise

key: \(p\): Jones ill    \(q\): Smith away    \(r\): Robinson comes to senses    \(s\): deal completed    \(t\): dividend declared

Build it from the outside in: the main connective is the first ‘if…then’, ‘unless’ gives the inner conditional, and ‘neither…nor…’ gives the conjunction of two negations at the very center.

translate it: the conclusion

key: \(p\): Jones ill    \(q\): Smith away    \(r\): Robinson comes to senses    \(s\): deal completed    \(t\): dividend declared

the argument, fully translated

  1. \((p \vee q) \to (\neg r \to (\neg s \wedge \neg t))\)
  1. \((q \wedge \neg r) \to \neg s\)

Recap slide — the premise and conclusion just checked live, gathered together.

why a full table won’t help

This argument has five propositional variables: \(p, q, r, s, t\).

A full truth table needs \(2^5 = 32\) rows — too many to fill out by hand.

Instead, we look directly for a counterexample: an assignment making the premise true and the conclusion false. If none exists, the argument is valid.

This is the pivot of the whole lecture: the truth-table idea (check every assignment) still works even when the truth-table artifact becomes impractical.

searching for a counterexample

To make the conclusion \((q \wedge \neg r) \to \neg s\) false, we need:

  • \(q\) true, \(r\) false (to make \(q \wedge \neg r\) true)
  • \(s\) true (to make \(\neg s\) false)

But then, in the premise, \(q\) true already makes \((p \vee q)\) true, and \(s\) true makes \((\neg s \wedge \neg t)\) false — so \(\neg r \to (\neg s \wedge \neg t)\) comes out false, forcing the whole premise false.

No assignment makes the premise true and the conclusion false — the argument is valid.

The key move: once q, r, and s are pinned down by the conclusion, p and t are irrelevant — the premise is forced false no matter what they are. That’s what makes this a proof of validity rather than just one failed attempt at a counterexample.

For the patient: the full 32-row table agrees with the reasoning above.

worked example: the horse race

Adapted from Resnik. Another five-variable, 32-row case — same technique as the Argus deal, this time landing on validity for a different structural reason (a flat-out contradiction rather than a forced-false premise).

the argument

If the horse loses a shoe, the owner will shoe the horse or hire someone to do so. If the owner shoes the horse, the owner will have a sore back. The race will be canceled if the owner has a sore back. So, if the horse loses a shoe and the owner does not hire someone to shoe the horse, the race will be canceled.

Three short conditionals building a chain, then a conclusion that combines two of the antecedents. Good preparation for the reasoning on the next few slides.

in premise-conclusion form

  1. If the horse loses a shoe, then the owner will shoe the horse or hire someone to do so.
  2. If the owner shoes the horse, then the owner will have a sore back.
  3. The race will be canceled if the owner has a sore back.
  1. If the horse loses a shoe and the owner does not hire someone to shoe the horse, the race will be canceled.

Premise 3 is a plain ‘if’ (not ‘only if’), so it paraphrases straightforwardly to ‘if sore back, then canceled’ despite the English order being reversed.

translation key

\(p\): horse loses a shoe

\(q\): owner shoes the horse

\(r\): owner hires someone

\(s\): owner has a sore back

\(t\): race is canceled

Now translate each line of the argument yourself:

Five variables again, but this time no ‘only if’ or ‘unless’ — a chance to see that length and variable count are the source of difficulty here, not exotic phrasing.

translate it: premise 1

key: \(p\): loses shoe    \(q\): owner shoes horse    \(r\): owner hires someone    \(s\): sore back    \(t\): race canceled

translate it: premise 2

key: \(p\): loses shoe    \(q\): owner shoes horse    \(r\): owner hires someone    \(s\): sore back    \(t\): race canceled

translate it: premise 3

key: \(p\): loses shoe    \(q\): owner shoes horse    \(r\): owner hires someone    \(s\): sore back    \(t\): race canceled

translate it: the conclusion

key: \(p\): loses shoe    \(q\): owner shoes horse    \(r\): owner hires someone    \(s\): sore back    \(t\): race canceled

the argument, fully translated

  1. \(p \to (q \vee r)\)
  2. \(q \to s\)
  3. \(s \to t\)
  1. \((p \wedge \neg r) \to t\)

Recap slide — the four answers just checked live, gathered into the finished argument.

why a full table won’t help

Five propositional variables again: \(p, q, r, s, t\) — a \(2^5 = 32\)-row table.

We look for a counterexample directly instead of filling out every row.

searching for a counterexample

To make the conclusion \((p \wedge \neg r) \to t\) false, we need:

  • \(p\) true, \(r\) false (to make \(p \wedge \neg r\) true)
  • \(t\) false

Chasing that through the premises:

  • premise 3 (\(s \to t\)) forces \(s\) false, or else it would be false
  • premise 2 (\(q \to s\)) then forces \(q\) false, or else it would be false
  • but now premise 1 (\(p \to (q \vee r)\)) has \(p\) true and both \(q, r\) false — so \(q \vee r\) is false, making premise 1 false

No assignment makes every premise true and the conclusion false — the argument is valid.

This is the chain-reaction style of counterexample search: falsifying the conclusion forces a cascade of forced values back through the premises until something breaks. Here, premise 1 is what breaks.

your turn: the accomplice

An unguided practice round, not a walked-through example. Same ‘only if’ nesting as the mind-and-brain argument, but this time it’s the students who translate — give them a few minutes before opening the worksheet.

the argument

If there are no signs of forced entry, then my client was the burglar only if she had an accomplice inside the building. My client didn’t have an accomplice inside the building. There are no signs of forced entry. So, my client was not the burglar.

Same cast of characters as the earlier burglar example, but note the explicit ‘only if’ in premise 1 in place of the earlier nested ‘if…then’ — worth asking the room whether that changes the logic (it doesn’t).

in premise-conclusion form

  1. If there are no signs of forced entry, then my client was the burglar only if she had an accomplice inside the building.
  2. My client did not have an accomplice inside the building.
  3. There are no signs of forced entry.
  1. My client was not the burglar.

Next time

Both large arguments today turned out valid, but for different reasons: one because a value was forced false by the premise’s own structure, the other because the chase for a counterexample ran into a flat contradiction. Watch for that difference — it comes up again when we look at proofs.
