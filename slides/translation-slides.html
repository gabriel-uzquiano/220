translation

We know how to use truth tables to assess the validity of arguments already written in the language of propositional logic.

One minute. Set up the gap: truth tables work on formulas, but the arguments we actually meet are in English. Translation is the bridge.

but arguments come to us in English

To assess the validity of a natural language argument, we first need to translate its premises and conclusion into the language of propositional logic.

Only then can we run a truth table on it.

Emphasize the order of operations: translate first, test second. Today is entirely about the first step.

the plan

Three steps, applied to each sentence in turn:

  1. paraphrase the sentence to reveal how it is built from simpler sentences by truth-functional connectives — ‘not’, ‘and’, ‘or’, ‘if … then …’
  2. replace each connective with its formal counterpart
  3. replace the simplest sentences that remain with propositional variables

Preview only — do not dwell. The two worked examples that follow are where this actually lands.

worked example: the British are coming

Paul Revere’s ride. A classic, and useful precisely because the English is loaded with connectives.

the argument

The British are coming by sea. For if they are coming by land, then Paul Revere should light two lamps. And if the British are not coming by land, then they are coming by sea. However, Paul Revere did not light two lamps.

Read it aloud once before showing the premise-conclusion form on the next slide.

in premise-conclusion form

  1. If the British are coming by land, Paul Revere should light two lamps.
  2. If the British are not coming by land, then they are coming by sea.
  3. Paul Revere did not light two lamps.
  1. The British are coming by sea.

This is the target. Everything on the next three slides is about turning these four sentences into formulas.

step 1 — paraphrase

We use parentheses to reveal how each sentence is built from simpler ones.

  1. (If the British are coming by land, then Paul Revere is supposed to light two lamps).
  2. (If the British are not coming by land, then they are coming by sea.)
  3. Paul Revere did not light two lamps.
  4. The British are coming by sea.

Nothing has changed in meaning — we are only making the grammatical skeleton visible. Point at ‘if’, ‘not’, ‘then’ as they light up.

step 2 — propositional connectives

We replace each connective with its formal counterpart:

English Symbol
not \(\neg\)
and \(\wedge\)
or \(\vee\)
if …, then … \(\to\)

Same table as the formal-languages deck. Worth a thirty-second recall before applying it.

step 2 continued — applying it

  1. (The British are coming by land \(\to\) Paul Revere will light two lamps).
  2. (\(\neg\) The British are coming by land \(\to\) The British are coming by sea).
  3. \(\neg\) Paul Revere lit two lamps.
  4. The British are coming by sea.

Each line is a direct substitution from the previous slide — no new decisions being made here yet.

step 3 — propositional variables

translation key

\(p\): The British are coming by land

\(q\): Paul Revere lights two lamps

\(r\): The British are coming by sea

Now translate each line of the argument yourself:

Ask the room to double check each line against the key before moving on — this is the step where a mismatched variable does the most damage.

We now have an argument in the language of propositional logic, so the truth table method applies directly.

worked example: interest rates

Second pass, faster. The point now is fluency, not the method itself.

the argument

Interest rates will rise or they will stay the same. If interest rates rise, then inflation will not rise. But if they stay the same, then inflation will rise. Inflation will not rise. Therefore, interest rates will rise.

  1. Interest rates will rise or they will stay the same.
  2. If interest rates rise, then inflation will not rise.
  3. If they stay the same, then inflation will rise.
  4. Inflation will not rise.
  1. Interest rates will rise.

Give the room thirty seconds to spot the connectives themselves before you narrate them.

translating it

translation key

\(p\): Interest rates will rise

\(q\): Interest rates will stay the same

\(r\): Inflation will rise

Your turn — translate each line yourself:

key: \(p\): rates rise    \(q\): rates stay the same    \(r\): inflation rises

issues with translation

Some English phrases resist a single, mechanical paraphrase.

Tone shift here: the method from the first half is easy to state and hard to apply cleanly. That is the point of this section.

structural ambiguity

The British are coming and Paul Revere will light one lamp or he will light two lamps.

  • ((The British are coming and Paul Revere will light one lamp) or Paul Revere will light two lamps) — or is the main connective
  • (The British are coming and (Paul Revere will light one lamp or Paul Revere will light two lamps)) — and is the main connective

Both parses are grammatical. English does not disambiguate; we have to, and we have to say so out loud when it happens.

the two translations disagree

translation key: \(p\): The British are coming    \(q\): Paul Revere lights one lamp    \(r\): Paul Revere lights two lamps

\(((p \wedge q) \vee r)\)

\((p \wedge (q \vee r))\)


They are not equivalent: they diverge when \(p\) and \(q\) are false and \(r\) is true.

The whole reason we bother flagging the ambiguity: the two readings are not just stylistically different, they have different truth conditions.

practice: which connective is main?

Consider the sentence again:

The British are coming and Paul Revere will light one lamp or he will light two lamps.

Answer: and; or

The sentence is genuinely ambiguous between two parses — one where ‘and’ is the main connective, one where ‘or’ is. ‘Not’ does not occur in the sentence at all.

Let them try it before revealing. If everyone converges on one answer, ask what the other reading would sound like out loud.

neither … nor …

Paul Revere will light neither one lamp nor two lamps.

Two acceptable paraphrases, matching the same truth conditions:

  • (Paul Revere will not light one lamp and Paul Revere will not light two lamps)
  • It is not the case that (Paul Revere will light one or two lamps)

translation key: \(p\): Paul Revere lights one lamp    \(q\): Paul Revere lights two lamps

\[ (\neg p \wedge \neg q) \]

Both paraphrases are legitimate and translate to the same formula, up to a De Morgan equivalence — worth a passing mention if the room is ready for it, otherwise skip.

only if — and how it differs from if

only if

Paul Revere will light two lamps only if the British are coming by sea.

Paraphrase: if Paul Revere lights two lamps, then the British have come by sea.

Coming by sea is necessary for lighting two lamps.

\[ p \to q \]

if

Paul Revere will light two lamps if the British come by sea.

Paraphrase: if the British come by sea, then Paul Revere lights two lamps.

Coming by sea is sufficient for lighting two lamps.

\[ q \to p \]

The lottery analogy is the fastest way to make this land: ‘I will win the lottery only if I buy a ticket’ (necessary) versus ‘I will win the lottery if I buy a ticket’ (false — and obviously so, which is the giveaway that ‘if’ and ‘only if’ point in different directions).

if, and only if, …

Paul Revere will light two lamps if, and only if, the British are coming by sea.

A conjunction of the two conditionals from the previous slide:

  • (Paul Revere will light two lamps if the British are coming by sea) and (Paul Revere will light two lamps only if the British are coming by sea)

\[ (q \to p) \wedge (p \to q) \]

We abbreviate formulas of this shape with \(\leftrightarrow\): \[ (\varphi \leftrightarrow \psi) := (\varphi \to \psi) \wedge (\psi \to \varphi) \]

It is fine to translate directly using ↔︎ once the abbreviation has been introduced — flag that this is a defined shorthand, not a fifth primitive connective.

unless

Paul Revere will light one lamp unless the British are coming by sea.

translation key: \(p\): The British are coming by sea    \(q\): Paul Revere lights one lamp

Two equivalent paraphrases:

If the British are not coming by sea, then Paul Revere lights one lamp.

\[ \neg p \to q \]

Paul Revere lights one lamp or the British are coming by sea.

\[ p \vee q \]

Both are correct and, in fact, logically equivalent — a nice callback to the equivalence check from the ambiguity slide, this time confirming agreement rather than disagreement.

practice: translating ‘unless’

translation key: \(p\): the British are coming by land    \(q\): Paul Revere lights two lamps
