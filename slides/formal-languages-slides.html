Formal Languages

PHIL 220. Introduction to Logic · 2.1

Gabriel Uzquiano

formal languages

We know validity is a matter of form. Now we need somewhere to write forms down.

Frame the whole unit in one sentence: the last two sessions told us that validity depends on form, and English is not a good place to display form. Everything today is preparation for that claim.

use and mention

A first lesson about being careful with words.

Two things you can talk about

Los Angeles has more than eighty cities in its metropolitan area.

About a city.

‘Los Angeles’ consists of two words.

About a name.

Quotation marks make a word mention itself instead of doing its usual work.

Los Angeles does not consist of two words. ‘Los Angeles’ has no cities in it.

Say the two false sentences out loud — they get a laugh, and the laugh is the lesson. This distinction will matter in two places later: when we say what a formula is, and when we talk about substitution in quantificational logic.

Where do the quotation marks go?

Add quotation marks wherever they belong:

  1. I is a Roman numeral, and I am a philosopher.
  2. England is part of New England, but England is not part of New England.
  1. ‘I’ is a Roman numeral, and I am a philosopher.
  2. ‘England’ is part of ‘New England’, but England is not part of New England.

Do the second one on the board. The trick is that the first half is a claim about words — the string is literally contained in the longer string — and the second is a claim about geography. Same characters, different subject.

form and meaning

Which words does the form depend on?

The word that does the work

  1. \(p\) and \(q\)
  1. Therefore, \(q\)

This is valid, and it is valid because of what ‘and’ means. Replace ‘and’ with ‘or’ and it collapses:

  1. It is raining or it is snowing.
  1. Therefore, it is snowing.

The point to land: the letters can be replaced by anything at all, but the logical words cannot. A formal language is a language in which only the logical words are fixed.

Let’s check it

Premise \((p \wedge q)\); conclusion \(q\). Only the rows where the conclusion is false are shown.

Two rows come back, and in both of them the premise is false. So there is nowhere for a counterexample to live, and the verdict reads ‘Valid’. Contrast with the affirming-the-consequent search from last session, where the highlighted row was the counterexample.

Four connectives

\(\neg\) negation it is not the case that
\(\wedge\) conjunction and
\(\vee\) disjunction or
\(\to\) the conditional if …, then …

Everything in propositional logic is built from sentence letters and these four symbols.

Write each symbol on the board as you say it; they need to be able to draw them, not just recognise them. Flag now that \(\to\) is the one whose truth table will surprise them, and that we come back to it.

Their truth tables

Already loaded — press Evaluate to fill them in.

Do not press the button immediately. Ask them for the rows of \((p \vee q)\) first and see whether anyone gives you the exclusive reading — that sets up the syrup example at the end of the hour.

A form the tables settle

\((p \vee q)\) and \(\neg p\); therefore \(q\).

This is the form behind ‘my office number is even or odd; it is not even; so it is odd’. Once the connectives have tables, questions about validity stop being matters of judgement.

three things to ask

Syntax, semantics, pragmatics.

Three questions about any language

  • Syntax. Which strings of symbols count as sentences? A question with a precise answer and no reference to meaning.
  • Semantics. What do the sentences mean — and when are they true?
  • Pragmatics. What do speakers convey by uttering them on an occasion?

An example of the third: ‘some students passed’ suggests not all did, though it does not say so. That suggestion is pragmatic, and it is why some of our translations will feel slightly wrong at first.

The next session is entirely syntax. Semantics follows, and pragmatics is what we will keep having to set aside.

why bother?

Two reasons, and both are about English rather than about logic.

Reason one: only the form should matter

Would you like syrup on your pancakes or waffles?

  • ‘Or’ here excludes: you are being offered one or the other, not both.
  • Elsewhere it does not: ‘we are hiring someone with a degree in philosophy or mathematics’.

A formal language fixes one meaning per symbol. \(\vee\) will be the inclusive one — and we will say why that is the useful choice.

Reason two: English hides structure

  1. Everything is caused by something.
  2. Only God can cause everything.
  1. Therefore, God causes everything.

‘Everything is caused by something’ can mean:

  • for each thing, there is something that causes it; or
  • there is one thing that causes everything.

On the first reading the premise is plausible but the argument fails. On the second the argument works but the premise is what was to be proved.

This is the best argument on the syllabus for the whole enterprise. The fallacy is invisible in English and impossible to state in a formal language, because the two readings get different formulas. We will translate this one properly in November.

Structure with connectives too

The car starts if there is gas in the tank and you turn the ignition.

  • \((\text{gas} \wedge \text{ignition}) \to \text{starts}\)
  • \(\text{gas} \wedge (\text{ignition} \to \text{starts})\)

The brackets are not decoration. English left them out; the formal language will not let us.

Ask which one they meant. Then point out that the second one asserts that there is gas in the tank, which the English sentence plainly does not.
