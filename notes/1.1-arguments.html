why logic?

We reason to persuade someone to accept a claim on the basis of something they accept.

Coin Toss

Consider the puzzle below:

You and I toss a coin, but we have no access to the other’s coin. We each make a guess as to the outcome of the other’s coin. We win if at least one of us makes a correct guess. However, we are allowed to agree on a common strategy before the coins are tossed.

What strategy should we use to make sure we win no matter what the outcomes of the tosses are?

It may be helpful to look at an illustration.

There is in fact one:

  • I will make my guess match the outcome of my coin.
  • You will make your guess differ from the outcome of your coin.
The Case for the Strategy:

Once we toss our coins, they will either match or not match. If they match, then my guess is guaranteed to succeed, since your coin will have landed in exactly the same way as mine. On the other hand, if they do not match, then your guess is guaranteed to succeed, since your coin will have landed differently from mine. Either way, one of our guesses will be successful, which means we will win the game.

Here is a more explicit formulation of the case for the strategy:

  1. Either the outcomes of our respective tosses will match or they will not match.
  2. If they match, then since I guess the same outcome as that I observed in my coin, I’m guaranteed to succeed.
  3. If I succeed, then at least one of us does and we win the game.
  4. If they do not match, then since you guess a different outcome from the one you observed in your coin, your guess is guaranteed to succeed.
  5. If you succeed, then at least one of us does and we win the game.
  6. So, at least one of us will succeed and we win the game.

Monty Hall

Consider the Monty Hall Problem for which there is a simple interactive demonstration.

There are three doors \(\#1\), \(\#2\), and \(\#3\), one with a prize behind it. You may first pick one of the three doors at random. You pick door \(\#1\), but you are not told yet whether there is a prize behind it.

The host now opens one of the other two doors being careful to select one without a prize behind it, say the host opens door \(\#3\).

At this point, you cannot yet know whether the prize is behind door \(\#1\) or door \(\#2\).

“Do you want to switch to door \(\#2\)?” the host asks.

What should you do?

The Case against the Switch

Once the host opens door \(\#3\) without a prize, there is a one in two chance door \(\#1\) is the winner and a one in two chance the prize is behind door \(\#2\). That is, both doors have the same chance of being the winner. But if both have the same chance of being the winner, then there is nothing to gain from the switch to the other door. So, there is nothing to gain from the switch to the other door.

Here is a more explicit formulation of the case:

  1. Both door \(\#1\) and door \(\#2\) have the same chance of being the winner.

  2. If both doors have the same chance of being the winner, then there is nothing to gain from the switch from one to the other.

  3. So, there is nothing to gain from the switch from one to the other.

The Case for the Switch

Once the host opens door \(\#3\) without a prize, the chance that door \(\#2\) is the winner is the same as the chance door \(\#1\) had no prize behind it. There was a two in three chance door \(\# 1\) had no prize behind it. That means that there is a two in three chance door \(\#2\) is the winner, which is greater than the chance door \(\#1\) is the winner. But if door \(\#2\) has a greater chance of being the winner, then you should switch. So, you should switch.

Here is the case reformulated:

  1. The chance that door \(\#2\) is the winner is the same as the chance door \(\#1\) had no prize behind it.
  2. There is a two in three chance door \(\#1\) is not the winner .
  3. If the chance that door \(\#2\) is the winner is the same as the chance door \(\#1\) had no prize behind it, then there is a two in three chance door \(\#2\) is the winner.
  4. There is a two in three chance door \(\#2\) is the winner .
  5. If there is a two in three chance door \(\#2\) is the winner , then the chance that door \(\#2\) is the winner is greater than the chance door \(\#1\) is the winner .
  6. The chance that \(\#2\) is the winner is greater than the chance door \(\#1\) is the winner.
  7. If the chance that \(\#2\) is the winner is greater than the chance door \(\#1\) is the winner, then you should switch.
  8. Therefore, you should switch.

The goal in each case is to persuade you of the truth of a statement on the basis of other statements you may already accept. Whether they succeed will depend partly on whether the claim in question follows from the claims you are supposed to accept and partly on whether you in fact accept them.

Or consider Galileo’s paradox, which attempts to persuade you of the truth of a statement you may have initially been inclined to reject on the basis of claims you should presumably accept.

Galileo’s Paradox

Are there more natural numbers such as 1, 2, 3, etc than perfect squares such as 1, 4, 9, etc?

The Case for More

Every perfect square is a natural number. However, not every natural number is a perfect square, for example, 2, 3, 5, 7, etc are not the square of any natural number. If every perfect square is a natural number but some natural numbers are not perfect squares, then there are strictly more natural numbers than perfect squares. So, there are strictly more natural numbers than perfect squares.

The Case for No More

There is a way to match natural numbers with perfect squares in a one-one fashion, for example, \(n^2 \mapsto n\). If there is a way to match natural numbers with perfect squares in a one-one fashion, then there are no more natural numbers than perfect squares. So, there are no more natural numbers than there are perfect squares.

Logic is concerned with the question of what follows from what. Sometimes a statement does not follow from the antecedently given statements and we rely on logic for an explanation.

The Case for a Gift Card

A gift card is better than a postcard. A poscard is better than nothing. Therefore, a gift card is better than nothing.

A gift card is better than nothing. Nothing is better than a yacht. So, a gift card is better than a yacht.

arguments

When we reason, we move from a body of statements, which we call premises, to another statement they are supposed to support, which we call the conclusion.

Example

Door \(\#1\) has the same chance of being the winner as door \(\#2\). But if door \(\#1\) has the same chance of being the winner as door \(\#2\), then there is nothing ot be gained from the switch to \(\#2\) So, there is nothing to be gained from the switch to \(\#2\).

In this case, there are two premises:

  1. Door \(\#1\) has the same chance of being the winner as door \(\#2\).
  2. If door \(\#1\) has the same chance of being the winner as door \(\#2\), then there is nothing to be gained from the switch to \(\#2\)

The conclusion, which is marked by the use of the phrase ‘so,’ is the statement:

  1. There is nothing to be gained from the switch to \(\#2\).
Example

A gift card is better than a postcard. A postcard is better than nothing. Therefore, a gift card is better than nothing.

In this case, there are two premises:

  1. A gift card is better than a postcard.
  2. A postcard is better than nothing

The conclusion, which is marked by the use of the phrase ‘therefore,’ is the statement:

  1. A gift card is better than nothing.
Example

If my client had been the burglar, she would have had to break in through an unlocked window in the second floor. My client couldn’t have climbed to the the second floor. So, my client is not the burglar.

In this case, we have two premises:

  1. If my client had been the burglar, she would have had to break in through an unlocked window in the second floor.
  2. My client couldn’t have climbed to the second floor.

The conclusion, which is preceded by the phrase ‘so’, is the statement:

  1. My client is not the burglar.
Definition

An argument is a sequence of declarative sentences, which are the premises, and a declarative sentence marked as the conclusion.

We use declarative sentences to make statements, which are true or false. There is a contrast between them and interrogative and imperative sentences, which are used to ask questions and issue commands, respectively.

Which of the sentences below are declarative sentences of English?

Answer: Whales live underwater; If whales live underwater, then some mammals do; You will shut the door

The premises are meant to support the conclusion, but there is no set order in which premises and conclusion occur in an argument. The conclusion is generally marked by occurrences of phrases such as ‘so’, ‘thus’, ‘therefore’, ‘it follows that’, etc, which indicate support from the premises.

Example

The laptop will not work if the battery is dead. The battery is dead. So, the laptop will not work.

The laptop will not work if the battery is dead. So, the laptop will not work. That is because the battery is dead.

The laptop will not work. For the laptop will not work if the battery is dead. And, moreover, the battery is dead.

It takes some skill to extract an argument from a paragraph, which we will use in the remainder of the course.

how to extract an argument from a paragraph

Argument A

If there is genuine change, then events must be future, then present, then past. If something is future, then it is neither present nor past. Nothing is future, present, and past. So, there is no genuine change.

Answer: There is no genuine change.
Argument B

If I speak both English and Spanish, then I’m bilingual. Therefore, I’m bilingual. For after all, I speak both English and Spanish.

Answer: I'm bilingual
Argument C

Time travel is impossible. For if time travel were possible, then a time traveler could change the past. Nothing, however, can change the past.

Answer: Time travel is impossible.

validity

Some arguments seem risk-free in that the truth of their premises automatically guarantees the truth of its conclusion. That seems to be the case with two of the four arguments below but not with the other two. Which ones?

Whales live underwater. Few mammals live underwater. Therefore, whales are not mammals.

People are now carrying umbrellas on the street. Therefore, it must be raining.

All Things Considered is a radio program. All radio programs are broadcast at least once a week. So, All Things Considered is broadcast at least once a week.

No one carries an umbrella on the street unless it is raining. Someone is now carrying an umbrella on the street. Therefore, it is raining.

We are interested in the contrast between what we have called risk-free arguments where the truth of the premises is automatically guaranteed by the truth of the premises and arguments for which some risk remains, no matter how minimal, that the premises may be true while the conclusion is false.

I bought ninety tickets for a fair raffle with just one hundred tickets. Therefore, I will win the raffle.

In this case, there is at least a one percent chance that the premise may be true and the conclusion false, which is not the case with the arguments below:

  1. If there is gas in the tank, then the engine will start. There is gas in the tank. Therefore, the engine will start.

  2. If today is Thursday, then tomorrow will be Friday. Today is Thursday. Therefore, tomorrow will be Friday.

  3. If the animal in the pen is a zebra, then it is not a cleverly painted mule. The animal in the pen is a zebra. Therefore, the animal in the pen is not a cleverly painted mule.

  4. The battery is dead or the laptop is broken. The battery is not dead. Therefore, the laptop is broken.

  5. Either the butler did it or the gardener did it. The butler did not do it. So, the gardener did it.

  6. Either interest rates will be raised further or inflation will continue to rise. Interest rates will not be raised further. Therefore, inflation will continue to rise.

The reason these arguments are risk-free is that they exemplify certain patterns:

  • Arguments 1, 2, and 3 all exemplify the pattern:

    1. If \(p\), then \(q\).
    2. \(p\).
    3. Therefore, \(q\).

  • Arguments 4, 5, and 6 all exemplify the pattern:

    1. \(p\) or \(q\)
    2. Not \(p\)
    3. \(q\)

In these case, \(p\) and \(q\) are placeholders for English declarative sentences.

What these argument patterns or forms have in common is that they never take one from true premises to a false conclusion. In what follows, we will be interested in what we will call a valid argument form, which we will use in order to characterize the validity of an argument.

Definition

An argument is valid if, and only if, it is an instance of a valid argument form.

It is the task of formal logic to offer a more precise characterization of a valid argument form. For the time being, we will content ourselves with the observation that valid forms exemplify the following features:

  1. A valid argument form has no argument instances with true premises and a false conclusion.

  2. A valid argument form preserves the truth of the premises regardless of subject matter.

Given what we know, we can dismiss an argument form as invalid if we can find some instances with true premises and a false conclusion.

Example

The following argument form is invalid:

  1. \(p\) if, and only if, \(q\)
  2. \(p\) and \(q\)

To appreciate that, it suffices to provide an instance with true premises and a false conclusion:

  1. Mark Twain wrote Hamlet if, and only if, Samuel Clemens did.

  2. Mark Twain wrote Hamlet and Samuel Clements did.

In this case, the premise is true because Mark Twain is the same individual as Samuel Clemens, but the conclusion is false because neither wrote Hamlet.

Example

The following argument form is invalid:

  1. If \(p\) and \(q\), then \(r\)
  2. \(p\)
  3. \(r\)

To appreciate the invalidity of this argument pattern, we can provide an instance with true premises and a false conclusion.

  1. If you turn the ignition and the tank is full, then the car will start.
  2. You turn the ignition.
  3. The car will start.

This argument will do provided it is given in a context in which the tank is not full and the car does not start.
